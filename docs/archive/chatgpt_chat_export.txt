Project 1 — Explainable, Learner-Controlled Adaptive Micro-Tutor
The idea
Build a small middle-school math tutor that adapts along two separate dimensions:
Academic adaptation: what problem, difficulty, hint, or worked example should come next?
Access adaptation: how should the activity be presented so the learner can work with it effectively?
The key idea is that these should not be the same model.
A learner getting several questions wrong may need a different academic problem. A learner who wants less text, no timer, a worked example, audio instructions, or smaller chunks may need a different presentation. One should not automatically be interpreted as the other.
This could be designed with neurodivergent learners in mind without requiring a diagnosis or claiming to identify one.
How it could work
The tutor could contain a small curriculum unit such as fractions, ratios, or introductory algebra.
A mastery model estimates what concepts the learner seems to understand from their problem-solving history. Bayesian Knowledge Tracing, Performance Factors, IRT/Elo-style models, or a small sequence model could be compared.
Separately, an access/preference model stores things such as preferred amount of text, whether instructions should be read aloud, hint granularity, timer visibility, chunk size, break preference, animation level, or preferred representation.
The tutor might say:

“You’ve used worked examples on the last two multi-step problems. Want one here?”

or:

“This problem has several instructions. Want me to show them one step at a time?”

The important part is that the learner can say yes, no, undo, or change the setting themselves.
The AI is therefore recommending rather than silently deciding.
Why this seems strong
There are many precedents for pieces of this idea. Systems such as Calcularis adapt academic instruction; iRead used learner/domain models to personalize literacy applications; accessibility standards already describe learner needs and alternative representations.
What seems less established is putting these together as two intentionally separate adaptive systems, with accessibility adaptation remaining transparent and learner-controlled.
That gives us a cleaner contribution than claiming to have invented adaptive tutoring.
It also creates a meaningful research question:

Does adding explainable, learner-controlled access adaptation to an academically adaptive tutor reduce learning friction or improve learning/agency compared with academic adaptation alone?

This feels particularly appropriate for AI for Social Impact because the question is not just whether the model predicts well. The question is whether the AI changes access to an educational service in a useful and responsible way.
What we should not claim
This would not “treat ADHD,” “identify dyslexia,” or discover an “autistic learning style.”
The stronger claim is that learners vary in both knowledge state and access needs, and a learning system may work better when it models those separately.

Project 2 — Adaptive Executive-Function Scaffold for Academic Work
The idea
Instead of building an entire adaptive tutor, build an AI layer that helps learners manage an academic task.
The target problem is executive-function friction: starting a task, keeping instructions available, breaking a problem into manageable pieces, deciding when to ask for help, remembering where you were, and managing time or breaks.
This is relevant to ADHD and other neurodivergent populations, but we would deliberately make the tool diagnosis-free. Anyone who benefits from external structure could use it.
How it could work
Imagine a learner solving a multi-step math problem.
The system could offer optional supports such as:
“Break this into steps.”
“Keep the instructions visible.”
“Show me only the current step.”
“Give me a worked example.”
“Hide the timer.”
“Save where I am and take a break.”
The interesting AI problem is deciding when a support might be useful without automatically imposing it.
For an initial version, the decision rule could actually be fairly simple and interpretable. Signals might include repeated errors, repeated reopening of instructions, hint requests, abandoned steps, or the learner explicitly saying “I’m stuck.”
A more advanced version could eventually learn which suggestions an individual learner tends to accept and which are actually helpful.
Again, the distinction matters: the model is estimating whether a support might help right now, not estimating whether the learner “has ADHD.”
Why this seems strong
This is narrower than Project 1, which could be an advantage.
There is a substantial literature on computerized cognitive training for ADHD. The important result is actually a cautionary one: training working memory can improve performance on closely related tasks without necessarily producing broad academic improvements.
That suggests a different social-impact approach.
Instead of trying to “train executive function” in an isolated game, support executive functioning inside the academic activity where the difficulty actually occurs.
The research question becomes something like:

Can context-aware, learner-controlled scaffolding reduce avoidable friction during authentic academic tasks without increasing dependence on the system?

This could potentially be tested without building a sophisticated curriculum model.
What makes the AI interesting
The long-term technical problem is a contextual intervention problem:
Given what is happening right now, should the system offer a particular support, offer nothing, or ask the learner?
That potentially connects to contextual bandits, adaptive interventions, recommender systems, and human-in-the-loop AI.
But we would not need reinforcement learning in the first version. A transparent model could be a stronger project if the evaluation is good.
Main risk
We have to distinguish helpful scaffolding from simply making everything easier.
The goal is not maximum assistance. The goal is the right amount of assistance while preserving autonomy and learning.

Project 3 — Learner-Controlled Accessibility / Equivalent-Content Engine
The idea
This moves further away from “AI tutor” and focuses directly on accessibility infrastructure.
The problem is that educational material is usually delivered in one primary representation. A learner might understand the underlying concept perfectly well but struggle with the way it is presented.
For example, the same concept might have:
standard explanation → concise explanation → read-aloud version → diagram → worked example → manipulable step-by-step representation
The system would maintain a portable learner preference profile and recommend an equivalent representation depending on the learner's stated preferences and current context.
How it could work
Suppose the learner reaches an algebra problem with a long text explanation.
The system knows that this learner often chooses diagrams and concise instructions for math. Instead of silently replacing the material, it could offer:

“There’s also a diagram and step-by-step version. Switch?”

The learner's preferences could persist across activities.
This idea has a particularly strong precedent in 1EdTech AccessForAll, which describes Personal Needs and Preferences and alternative/equivalent resources. Importantly, the standard focuses on what support someone needs rather than why they need it.
That maps almost exactly onto the direction we have been discussing.
Where AI enters
There are several possible levels.
At the simplest level, there may be almost no AI: the learner explicitly configures their preferences and the system matches them to content.
A more interesting version could learn which representations tend to help in which contexts and recommend rather than automatically switch.
For example:

learner + concept + task characteristics + previous choices → recommended representation

Over time this becomes a recommender-system problem.
A generative model could also help create alternative representations, but I would be careful about making LLM content generation the central contribution. Generated educational content introduces correctness and equivalence problems that could distract from the stronger research question.
Why this could matter
This is probably the most directly accessibility-focused project.
Most personalization systems ask:

“What should this learner learn next?”

This project asks:

“How can the learner access the same learning objective in a form that works for them?”

That is a different personalization problem.
It also potentially extends beyond neurodivergence. The same architecture could support visual, motor, language, cognitive, temporary, environmental, or assistive-technology needs.
Main question

Can a learner-controlled recommendation system reduce the effort required to find useful accessibility adaptations while preserving choice and avoiding diagnosis-based personalization?

This could potentially become infrastructure used by an existing educational system rather than another standalone tutoring product.

Project 4 — Robust Knowledge Tracing for Real Learners
The idea
This is the more technical/research-heavy fallback if access to schools, children, or a partner becomes difficult.
Instead of claiming a new tutoring system, ask whether current student-modeling methods are actually reliable enough to drive high-stakes personalization.
Knowledge-tracing models estimate what a student knows from their history of answers. Those estimates can determine what problem or support a tutor gives next.
That makes a basic question surprisingly important:

How robust, calibrated, and fair are these models when used under realistic conditions?

How it could work
We could use FoundationalASSIST, a recent dataset built from authentic ASSISTments middle-school math activity. It contains a large set of real student/problem interactions and the actual natural-language problems and responses.
We could compare several levels of modeling:
simple statistical baseline → BKT/PFA-style model → neural knowledge tracing
But the contribution would not just be “model X gets a slightly higher AUC.”
The interesting analysis would be what happens with:
unseen students, students with very little history, rare problems, unseen content, missing interaction data, different achievement levels, and model confidence/calibration.
A tutor making an incorrect prediction with 55% confidence is very different from a tutor making the same incorrect prediction with 99% confidence.
We could also test whether apparently sophisticated models still beat simple interpretable baselines once the evaluation prevents leakage between students and respects chronological order.
Why this fits the broader project
Projects 1–3 all assume that some model can estimate learner state well enough to make useful decisions.
This project challenges that assumption directly.
It also creates an interesting responsible-AI contribution: if an adaptive educational system performs significantly worse for students with short histories or unusual interaction patterns, those learners may receive systematically worse personalization.
The project could therefore connect knowledge tracing, uncertainty, calibration, robustness, and equitable access to adaptive education.
What it cannot show
An offline dataset can tell us whether a model predicts future answers.
It generally cannot tell us:

“If the tutor had selected a different problem, would the student have learned more?”

That is a causal intervention question and would require stronger experimental evidence.
Keeping that distinction explicit could itself make this a stronger project.

How I currently see the options

Five substantive methodological issues
A. Mathematical equivalence needs more than matching numbers.
Suppose an original problem says:

Mia has four boxes with six pencils in each box. How many pencils does she have altogether?

A valid chunked version could say:

Mia has four boxes. Each box contains six pencils. How many pencils does Mia have altogether?

Mia has four boxes. Each box contains six pencils. Multiply four by six to find the total.

It preserves the correct answer but adds an operation hint.

Meaning



Answer preserved
Same numerical answer


Mathematical structure preserved
Same quantities, entities, relationships, conditions, and target


Instructional demand preserved
No added strategy, intermediate result, or answer cue


Presentation requirement satisfied
Genuinely simpler wording or better chunking

This is especially important for chunking. Organizing information under “Given,” “Step 1,” and “Step 2” could unintentionally tell the learner what mathematical operations to perform.

C. The project needs a rule for when no rewrite is necessary.
A short, clear problem may not need simplification. The correct model behavior might be to leave it unchanged.
I would add an annotation label:
Adaptation needed? Yes / No / Unclear
Then evaluate whether the model makes unnecessary edits. This creates a valuable additional research question: Can the system recognize when not to modify educational content?
D. One hundred problems are enough for an informative benchmark, but not rare-error guarantees.
There are 400 generated versions, but only 100 distinct source problems. Outcomes from different transformations of the same source are related, so the apparent sample size should not be treated as 400 independent problems.
The proposal's plan to resample at the original-problem level is correct. I would also report per-model/per-format raw counts and confidence intervals. Failure-type comparisons should remain exploratory.
E. GSM8K is a convenience benchmark, not an accessibility dataset.
The official dataset has 7,473 training and 1,319 test problems in its main configuration and is MIT-licensed. It provides mathematical questions and worked solutions, but no labels for reading barriers or learner preferences.

That additional set would meaningfully strengthen generalizability without turning the project into a full data-collection study.

7. Does it have a genuine social-impact justification?
Yes—but the claim must be carefully bounded.
Research provides evidence that reading and language processing are relevant to mathematical word-problem performance. For example, Fuchs et al. studied 325 second graders and found that language and text comprehension were important predictors of word-problem outcomes. This supports the existence of a language-related educational barrier; it does not show that Kathy's exact simplifications help neurodivergent learners. 
There is also a credible institutional use case: teachers cannot manually verify every automatically generated version of every problem.
The social-impact story becomes:

Students may face unnecessary language and presentation barriers in mathematics. LLMs could help educators produce accessible alternatives at scale, but inaccurate rewrites can silently change what learners are asked to solve. We will investigate how reliably these adaptations preserve mathematical and instructional meaning, and whether automated verification can make them safer for educator review.

This is an education-equity and responsible-AI problem, but the deliverable is an upstream safety evaluation, not a demonstrated accessibility intervention.
What about neurodivergence?
I would absolutely retain neurodivergent learners as part of the motivation, particularly the importance of choice and variation in reading and information-processing needs.
But I would not describe the benchmark as “validated for neurodivergent learners” or claim it improves ADHD, dyslexia, or autism outcomes. The planned study includes no such users or outcome measures, and neither plain language nor chunking is universally beneficial to people with those diagnoses.
A better framing is neurodiversity-informed and accessibility-motivated, without diagnosis-specific efficacy claims.

My preferred title is:
When Does Simplification Change the Math? Evaluating and Verifying LLM-Generated Accessible Word Problems
Other good choices are Preserving Mathematical Meaning in AI-Adapted Educational Content and SafeMathAdapt: A Benchmark for Mathematical Fidelity and Instructional Leakage in LLM Rewrites.
For the October 14 teaching-staff discussion, I would keep GSM8K as the primary dataset because the license, scale, and available solutions make it a pragmatic choice. However, the proposal needs to explain that the benchmark measures fidelity, not comprehension or accessibility effectiveness.
I would also add the following highly relevant recent source to the essential reading set:
Mirzadeh et al., GSM-Symbolic: Understanding the Limitations of Mathematical Reasoning in Large Language Models (2024). It shows that model performance can be sensitive to changes in numerical values and wording/context around GSM-style math problems. This is relevant background for why mathematical semantics matter, though solver instability should not itself be treated as proof that a rewrite changed the problem. 
For the teaching staff, the critical three papers to foreground are Norberg et al. (2025), Agrawal & Carpuat (2024), and EDUMATH (2026). Together they demonstrate the prior work on actual math rewriting, meaning-preservation evaluation, and teacher-reviewed math-generation benchmarks.

9. What I would recommend we tell Kathy
I would say that we should move ahead with her narrower proposal, while strengthening three parts before finalizing it.
First, cite Norberg et al. (2025) prominently and clearly state what is different. Second, make mathematical-task preservation and unwanted assistance the central contribution—not just readability or which model wins. Third, add a lightweight verification/revision component if time permits, since that turns the project from measuring known risks into investigating how to mitigate them.
I would not insist on building AccessPath at the same time. A polished benchmark plus failure detector, rigorous evaluation, and a small optional demo is a far better two-person deliverable than two partially completed projects.
My final call: Kathy's direction is the right practical choice for this course, but I would rename and reposition it around trustworthy educational adaptation. Its strongest question is not whether LLMs can make a problem sound simpler. It is whether they can do so without corrupting the learning task—and whether we can reliably catch failures before students encounter them.

I would add an annotation label:
Adaptation needed? Yes / No / Unclear
Then evaluate whether the model makes unnecessary edits. This creates a valuable additional research question: Can the system recognize when not to modify educational content?
D. One hundred problems are enough for an informative benchmark, but not rare-error guarantees.
There are 400 generated versions, but only 100 distinct source problems. Outcomes from different transformations of the same source are related, so the apparent sample size should not be treated as 400 independent problems.
The proposal's plan to resample at the original-problem level is correct. I would also report per-model/per-format raw counts and confidence intervals. Failure-type comparisons should remain exploratory.
E. GSM8K is a convenience benchmark, not an accessibility dataset.
The official dataset has 7,473 training and 1,319 test problems in its main configuration and is MIT-licensed. It provides mathematical questions and worked solutions, but no labels for reading barriers or learner preferences. 
Furthermore, GSM8K is widely used in model development and evaluation. Familiarity with these problems is a possible confound, so I would add 20–30 openly licensed, previously unused school-style problems as a small external check, if time permits.
That additional set would meaningfully strengthen generalizability without turning the project into a full data-collection study.

Recommended primary metric
Strict safe-adaptation rate
A rewritten problem counts as a safe adaptation only if all of the following hold:

mathematical conditions and requested quantity are preserved;
no ambiguity that changes the task is introduced;

percentage of unnecessary changes;
presentation compliance;
verifier detection recall and precision;

7. Does it have a genuine social-impact justification?
Yes—but the claim must be carefully bounded.
Research provides evidence that reading and language processing are relevant to mathematical word-problem performance. For example, Fuchs et al. studied 325 second graders and found that language and text comprehension were important predictors of word-problem outcomes. This supports the existence of a language-related educational barrier; it does not show that Kathy's exact simplifications help neurodivergent learners. 
There is also a credible institutional use case: teachers cannot manually verify every automatically generated version of every problem.
The social-impact story becomes:

Students may face unnecessary language and presentation barriers in mathematics. LLMs could help educators produce accessible alternatives at scale, but inaccurate rewrites can silently change what learners are asked to solve. We will investigate how reliably these adaptations preserve mathematical and instructional meaning, and whether automated verification can make them safer for educator review.

This is an education-equity and responsible-AI problem, but the deliverable is an upstream safety evaluation, not a demonstrated accessibility intervention.
What about neurodivergence?
I would absolutely retain neurodivergent learners as part of the motivation, particularly the importance of choice and variation in reading and information-processing needs.
But I would not describe the benchmark as “validated for neurodivergent learners” or claim it improves ADHD, dyslexia, or autism outcomes. The planned study includes no such users or outcome measures, and neither plain language nor chunking is universally beneficial to people with those diagnoses.
A better framing is neurodiversity-informed and accessibility-motivated, without diagnosis-specific efficacy claims.

It shows that model performance can be sensitive to changes in numerical values and wording/context around GSM-style math problems. This is relevant background for why mathematical semantics matter, though solver instability should not itself be treated as proof that a rewrite changed the problem. 
For the teaching staff, the critical three papers to foreground are Norberg et al. (2025), Agrawal & Carpuat (2024), and EDUMATH (2026). Together they demonstrate the prior work on actual math rewriting, meaning-preservation evaluation, and teacher-reviewed math-generation benchmarks.
